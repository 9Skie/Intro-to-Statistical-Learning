## Supervised Vs Unsupervised Learning

In supervised learning, each observation of the predictors $x_i$ has a corresponding response $y_i$. Our training data therefore consists of input–output pairs:

$\mathcal{D} = {(x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2), \ldots, (x_n,y_n)}$

We use these observations to estimate the relationship between the predictors and the response, obtaining a fitted model $\hat{f}$.

For a new input $x$, we predict its response using:

$\hat{y} = \hat{f}(x)$


In unsupervised learning, we observe the predictor measurements without corresponding response measurements:

$\mathcal{D} = {x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n}$

There is no designated response $Y$ that we are learning to predict, and therefore we therefore cannot learn an input–response relationship at all/
- Instead, we seek structure within the inputs, such as groups of similar observations or relationships among the predictors.

 One approach is **clustering**, which asks whether the observations form distinct groups based on similarities in their predictor measurements.

Clustering usually groups **observations**, rather than the predictor variables themselves. Each observation $x_i$ contains measurements of the predictors $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_p$. Observations with similar measurements may be assigned to the same group.

When there are two predictors, each observation can be plotted as a point with coordinates given by its measurements of $X_1$ and $X_2$. Groups of nearby points may indicate clusters. Well-separated groups are easier to identify, while overlapping groups make assignments more uncertain.

## Assess Model Accuracy

> [!PDF|yellow] [[An Introduction To Statistical Learning.pdf#page=37&selection=140,20,151,18&color=yellow|An Introduction To Statistical Learning, p.37]]
> >  Why is it necessary to introduce so many different statistical learning approaches, rather than just a single best method? There is no free lunch in statistics: no one method dominates all others over all possible data sets.


In order to evaluate how good our model is on a given data set, we need some math and return some numbers, if it's smaller, we should say our model's pretty close, if it's larger, our model sucks.
- This function defines our error, or sometimes called loss, it's the penalty to a prediction based on its discrepancy from the actual response.


A commonly used loss in regression is **squared error**:

$L(y_i,\hat{f}(x_i)) = [y_i-\hat{f}(x_i)]^2$


Averaging this loss across the observations gives the **mean squared error**, or MSE:

$\operatorname{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[y_i-\hat{f}(x_i)]^2$

- $n$ is the number of observations being evaluated.
- $y_i$ is the observed response for observation $i$.
- $\hat{f}(x_i)$ is the model’s predicted response for that observation.
- $y_i-\hat{f}(x_i)$ is the prediction error.


Squaring prevents positive and negative errors from cancelling each other out. It also penalizes large errors more heavily: doubling an error makes its squared contribution four times as large.

In practice, data is divided into training and testing data, as we 'train' our model on the training data, and evaluate the model on previously unforeseen (to the model) testing data.

Therefore, we would have training MSE, and testing MSE. A low training MSE does not guarantee a low test MSE. A highly flexible model may fit the training observations extremely closely, while making poor predictions on new observations.



Here's an example where there's good fitting on the training data, but poor generalization to the testing data.

## Bias Variance Tradeoff

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YIPsfEtJppE

Suppose we want to build a machine learning model that predicts whether a student will be admitted to medical school.

Imagine a large population of $N$ applicants. For each applicant, there are $p$ predictor measurements, such as GPA, MCAT score, extracurricular activities, and other recorded characteristics. The response $Y$ records whether the applicant was admitted.

In practice, we cannot collect data on every applicant. Instead, we receive a smaller training dataset containing $n$ observations and use it to fit our model.

However, **the fitted model depends on which observations we receive**.


If we receive training dataset $D_1$, we fit a model $\hat{f}_1$. If we receive another training dataset $D_2$, the same learning method produces another fitted model $\hat{f}_2$. 
- Repeating this process with $K$ possible training datasets produces $K$ fitted models.


Each model learns from its own training sample, but it should be able to generalize the patterns it observe and  make predictions for applicants outside that sample. 
- We can therefore give **the same applicant’s information $x_0$** to every fitted model and compare their predictions.

This lets us examine two different properties:
- **Variance:** how much the predictions change across training datasets.
- **Bias:** how far the average prediction across those datasets is from the true underlying target.


**First, consider a very simple learning method.**

Suppose our rule is to calculate the mean GPA in the training sample, predict admission for applicants above that threshold, and predict rejection for applicants below it.

This is a deliberately crude model that ignores other information, in other words, it's under-fitting.
- That is **high bias**, where the model's predictions are far from the truth.

However, suppose the training samples are sufficiently large and representative that their mean GPAs are similar. The fitted thresholds will then be similar, and the models will often give similar predictions for the same applicant.
- That is **low variance**: changing the training sample does not change the predictions very much.

The model can therefore be stable while still being systematically inaccurate.


**Now consider a much more flexible learning method.**

This method can use many applicant characteristics and represent complicated nonlinear relationships and interactions between them.

Its flexibility may allow it to capture important patterns that the simple threshold rule misses. 
- Across many training datasets, its average prediction may be closer to the underlying target, that means it can have **lower bias**.

However, it can also respond strongly to details that happen to appear in one particular training sample.
- Some of those details reflect genuine patterns. 
- Others reflect noise or chance associations that do not reliably occur in the wider population.

Consequently, a model trained on $D_1$ may make a substantially different prediction from a model trained on $D_2$, even when both are given exactly the same applicant information $x_0$.
- That is **high variance**: the fitted predictions are sensitive to the particular training data, if the data changes, the model correspondingly changes a lot, and the outputs change a lot.

If this sensitivity produces a closer fit to the training observations but worse predictions on new observations, the model is **overfitting**. 
- Its excellent training performance does not translate into equally good generalization.

A flexible method can therefore have low bias and high variance at the same time: its predictions may be close to the underlying target **on average**, while individual fitted models differ substantially depending on their training samples.


The bias–variance trade-off concerns these two sources of error.
- A model that is too restrictive may fail to capture important patterns, producing high bias.
- A model that is too sensitive to its training sample may follow accidental fluctuations, producing high variance.
- We want enough flexibility to capture useful structure while controlling sensitivity to the particular sample.

The big TLDR is:
- if model too simple, can't capture complicated pattern (under-fitting), it sucks at prediction.
- if model too complex, captures complicated pattern in given data too well (over-fitting), fails at generalization, it sucks at prediction

Therefore, we need model that's 'good enough'.



To describe the bias–variance trade-off mathematically, we hold one input $x_0$ fixed and imagine repeatedly training the same learning method on different datasets.

Let $D$ represent a training dataset. The model fitted using that dataset is $\hat{f}_D$, and its prediction at our fixed input is:

$\hat{f}_D(x_0)$

Changing the training dataset may change the fitted model and therefore its prediction, even though $x_0$ stays the same.


The actual response at this input is:

$Y_0 = f(x_0) + \epsilon_0$

- $f(x_0)$ is the true average outcome at $x_0$.
- $\epsilon_0$ is the variation of an individual outcome around that average.

We assume that $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_0]=0$ and that this new outcome’s noise is independent of the training dataset.


**First, consider the average prediction across training datasets.**

$\bar{f}(x_0) = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}_D(x_0)]$

The subscript $D$ means that we average over possible training datasets. Thus, $\bar{f}(x_0)$ is the average prediction made by the learning method when trained repeatedly on different samples.

- $f(x_0)$: the true average **outcome** at $x_0$.
- $\bar{f}(x_0)$: the average **model prediction** at $x_0$.
- 

**Bias measures the difference between these two averages.**

$\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{f}(x_0)) = \bar{f}(x_0)-f(x_0)$

- A large difference means that the learning method’s predictions are systematically off target, even after averaging across training datasets.

**Variance measures how much the fitted predictions differ from their own average.**

$\operatorname{Var}_D(\hat{f}_D(x_0)) = \mathbb{E}_D[(\hat{f}_D(x_0)-\bar{f}(x_0))^2]$

- If different training datasets produce similar predictions at $x_0$, variance is small. If their predictions differ substantially, variance is large.


We can now connect these quantities to **expected squared prediction error**:

$\mathbb{E}[(Y_0-\hat{f}_D(x_0))^2]$

This expectation averages over both the possible training datasets and the noise in the new outcome.

**First, separate the noise from the error in estimating $f$.**

$\mathbb{E}[(Y_0-\hat{f}_D(x_0))^2]$

$= \mathbb{E}[(f(x_0)-\hat{f}_D(x_0)+\epsilon_0)^2]$

$= \mathbb{E}_D[(f(x_0)-\hat{f}_D(x_0))^2] + 2\mathbb{E}[(f(x_0)-\hat{f}_D(x_0))\epsilon_0] + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_0^2]$

$= \mathbb{E}_D[(f(x_0)-\hat{f}_D(x_0))^2] + \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_0)$

The cross term is zero because the noise has mean zero and is independent of the training data. Its mean being zero also gives $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_0^2]=\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_0)$.

We have recovered **average reducible error plus irreducible error**.

**Next, split the reducible error into variance and squared bias.**

Add and subtract the average prediction:

$\hat{f}_D(x_0)-f(x_0) = [\hat{f}_D(x_0)-\bar{f}(x_0)] + [\bar{f}(x_0)-f(x_0)]$

The first bracket describes a fitted prediction’s deviation from the average prediction. The second bracket is the bias.

Squaring and averaging gives:

$\mathbb{E}_D[(\hat{f}_D(x_0)-f(x_0))^2]$

$= \mathbb{E}_D[(\hat{f}_D(x_0)-\bar{f}(x_0))^2] + 2[\bar{f}(x_0)-f(x_0)]\mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}_D(x_0)-\bar{f}(x_0)] + [\bar{f}(x_0)-f(x_0)]^2$

The middle term is zero because deviations from the average have average zero:

$\mathbb{E}_D[\hat{f}_D(x_0)-\bar{f}(x_0)] = 0$

Therefore:

$\mathbb{E}_D[(\hat{f}_D(x_0)-f(x_0))^2] = \operatorname{Var}_D(\hat{f}_D(x_0)) + [\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{f}(x_0))]^2$

Combining the two results:

$\mathbb{E}[(Y_0-\hat{f}_D(x_0))^2] = [\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{f}(x_0))]^2 + \operatorname{Var}_D(\hat{f}_D(x_0)) + \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_0)$

$\text{Expected test MSE at }x_0 = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Irreducible error}$

Increasing flexibility often decreases squared bias but increases variance. We want the flexibility that gives the smallest combined error. Even if both squared bias and model variance were zero, the irreducible error would remain.
