Assume we are experts invited to investigate the association between advertising and sales of a particular product.

> [!PDF|yellow] [[An Introduction To Statistical Learning.pdf#page=25&selection=37,37,42,16&color=yellow|An Introduction To Statistical Learning, p.25]]
> >  It is not possible for our client to directly increase sales of the product. On the other hand, they can control the advertising expenditure in each of the three media. Therefore, if we determine that there is an association between advertising and sales, then we can instruct our client to adjust advertising budgets, thereby indirectly increasing sales

Imagine we have records from many markets. For each market, we know:

- **Inputs, X:** spending on TV, radio, and newspaper advertising.
- **Outcome, Y:** the resulting sales.

We want to learn the relationship between those advertising budgets and sales. Then, given a new combination of budgets, we can predict sales.


![[An Introduction To Statistical Learning 3.jpg|500]]

[[An Introduction To Statistical Learning.pdf#page=26&rect=87,510,418,666&color=yellow|An Introduction To Statistical Learning, p.26]]



More formally, suppose we observe a quantitative response $Y$ and $p$ predictor variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_p$. We collect these predictors into a vector $X = (X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_p)$.

We assume that the response and predictors are related through the general model:

$Y = f(X) + \epsilon$

Here:

- $Y$ is the numerical outcome we want to predict.
- $X$ contains the information available for making that prediction.
- $f$ is the unknown function describing the systematic relationship between $X$ and $Y$.
- $\epsilon$ is an error term representing variation in $Y$ that $f(X)$ does not capture, such as unobserved influences or measurement error.

Given observed examples of inputs and outcomes, our goal is to **estimate $f$ from the data**. We denote this estimate by $\hat{f}$.

For a new input $x$, our predicted response is:

$\hat{y} = \hat{f}(x)$

Different regression methods make different assumptions about the form of $f$ and how to estimate it.


In our advertising example, we want to predict **sales from spending on TV, radio, and newspaper advertising**.

We can map this to the general regression model as follows:
- $Y$: sales.
- $X_1$: spending on TV advertising.
- $X_2$: spending on radio advertising.
- $X_3$: spending on newspaper advertising.

There are $p = 3$ predictors, collected into the vector $X = (X_1, X_2, X_3)$.

Our model is therefore:

$Y = f(X_1, X_2, X_3) + \epsilon$

-  $f$ describes the relationship between **all three advertising budgets together** and sales.
- $\epsilon$ (error term) captures variation in sales that these inputs do not explain, for example, differences in customer demand or competitors’ activity.


Our task is to use historical advertising budgets and their corresponding sales to estimate $f$, obtaining $\hat{f}$.

Given a new advertising budget $(x_1, x_2, x_3)$, we predict sales using:

$\hat{y} = \hat{f}(x_1, x_2, x_3)$

## Good Error, Bad Error

Based on observations of inputs $X$ and actual outcomes $Y$, we assume that their relationship follows:
- $Y = f(X) + \epsilon$

We do not directly know $f$. Instead, we use the observed data to estimate it, obtaining $\hat{f}$. We then predict the outcome using:
- $\hat{Y} = \hat{f}(X)$

Here, $\hat{f}$ is our learned model. It generally will not be a perfect estimate of $f$, and even knowing $f$ perfectly would not let us predict every outcome exactly because of $\epsilon$.

There are therefore two sources of prediction error:
- **Reducible error** comes from the difference between $\hat{f}$ and $f$. We can potentially reduce it through better learning methods or more useful training data.
- **Irreducible error** comes from the variation represented by $\epsilon$, which cannot be predicted using our chosen inputs $X$. Improving our estimate of $f$ alone cannot remove it, it's just 'there' in the natural world.


We can express this distinction mathematically by examining the **expected squared prediction error**:

$\mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2]$
- $Y = f(X) + \epsilon$ 
- $\hat{Y} = \hat{f}(X)$

This measures the average squared difference between the actual outcome and our prediction. 
- Squaring prevents positive and negative errors from cancelling each other out.

For this calculation, we hold both our fitted model $\hat{f}$ and the input $X$ fixed. The prediction $\hat{Y}$ is therefore fixed, and the only remaining variation in $Y$ comes from $\epsilon$. We assume that this noise has mean zero at the fixed input.

Substituting $Y = f(X) + \epsilon$ and $\hat{Y} = \hat{f}(X)$ gives:

$\mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2] = \mathbb{E}[(f(X) - \hat{f}(X) + \epsilon)^2]$

$\mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2] = [f(X) - \hat{f}(X)]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)$


![[An Introduction To Statistical Learning 1 1.jpg|500]]

[[An Introduction To Statistical Learning.pdf#page=28&rect=140,249,360,305&color=yellow|An Introduction To Statistical Learning, p.28]]

The two terms represent:
- **Reducible error:** $[f(X) - \hat{f}(X)]^2$, the squared difference between the true function and our estimated function at this input.
- **Irreducible error:** $\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)$, the variance of the remaining noise. Since the noise has mean zero, this equals its average squared size.


Even if our model perfectly estimates the function, so that $\hat{f}(X) = f(X)$, the expected squared prediction error is still:

$\mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)$

Therefore, the irreducible error sets a **lower bound on expected squared prediction error** for the available inputs:

$\mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2] \geq \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)$

## How to Guess $f$

Suppose we have a finite training dataset containing $n$ observations:
- $\mathcal{D} = {(x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2), \ldots, (x_n,y_n)}$
	-  $x_i$ is the vector of predictor values for observation $i$, and $y_i$ is its observed outcome.

For a particular target input $x_0$, we want to estimate the regression function:
- $f(x_0) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x_0]$
	- This is the **population average outcome among observations with input $x_0$**.

A natural approach would be to find all training observations whose inputs equal $x_0$ and average their outcomes. Define their index set as:
- $I(x_0) = {i : x_i = x_0}$

If this set contains observations, we could estimate the average using:
- $\hat{f}(x_0) = \frac{1}{|I(x_0)|}\sum_{i \in I(x_0)} y_i$
	-  $|I(x_0)|$ is the number of matching observations.


However, our finite dataset creates two problems:
- **No matching observations:** if $|I(x_0)| = 0$, we have nothing to average, and this formula is undefined.
- **Very few matching observations:** the average may be strongly affected by the particular noise in those observations, making it an unreliable estimate of $f(x_0)$.


Therefore, the estimation problem is:
- **How can we estimate $f(x_0)$ when we have too little data at exactly $x_0$ to calculate a reliable average?**
