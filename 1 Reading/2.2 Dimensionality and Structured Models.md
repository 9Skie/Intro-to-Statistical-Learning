## Parametric

A **parametric approach** estimates an unknown function $f$ by first choosing a mathematical form for it, then estimating the parameters within that form, which takes 2 steps.


**1. Assume a form for $f$.**

We choose a family of functions that we think can reasonably approximate the relationship between the predictors and the response.

For example, we might assume a linear form:

$f(X) \approx \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \cdots + \beta_pX_p$

The structure of the equation is specified, while the coefficients $\beta_0, \beta_1, \ldots, \beta_p$ remain unknown. These coefficients are the **parameters**.
- This assumption reduces the problem of estimating an unrestricted function $f$ to estimating $p+1$ numbers.


**2. Fit the parameters using the training data.**

Given observations ${(x_1,y_1), \ldots, (x_n,y_n)}$, we use a fitting procedure to estimate the parameters.

For the linear model, this produces:

$\hat{f}(X) = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1X_1 + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_pX_p$

The hats indicate that the coefficients have been estimated from data. Together, the chosen form and estimated coefficients define our fitted model $\hat{f}$.


One possible fitting procedure is **ordinary least squares**, which chooses coefficients that minimize the sum of squared differences between observed and predicted outcomes:

$\sum_{i=1}^{n}[y_i - \hat{f}(x_i)]^2$

The main advantage is that estimating a fixed set of parameters is generally easier than estimating an unrestricted function.

The main limitation is that the chosen form may not adequately represent the true relationship. Even well-estimated parameters can produce poor predictions if the assumed form is unsuitable.

**Parametric does not necessarily mean linear.** A nonlinear family can also be parametric. The defining idea is that we specify a family of functions described by a fixed number of parameters, then use data to estimate those parameters.

## Non-Parametric

A **non-parametric approach** estimates an unknown function $f$ without restricting it to a particular form described by a fixed number of coefficients, such as a linear equation. 
- Instead, it allows the shape of the estimated function $\hat{f}$ to adapt more flexibly to the observed data.


Given training observations ${(x_1,y_1), \ldots, (x_n,y_n)}$, we seek an estimate $\hat{f}$ that captures the underlying relationship and produces useful predictions:

$\hat{Y} = \hat{f}(X)$

The central challenge is to **fit the observed patterns while controlling the flexibility of the estimate**.

- If the estimate is too restrictive or too smooth, it may miss important features of the true relationship.
- If the estimate is too flexible, it may follow random fluctuations in the training data too closely.

This second problem is called **overfitting**. Since the observations follow $Y = f(X) + \epsilon$, fitting every observed point perfectly can mean fitting the noise $\epsilon$ as well as the underlying relationship $f$.
- Therefore, zero training error does not necessarily mean that $\hat{f}$ is a good estimate of $f$.

Many non-parametric methods have settings that control flexibility, such as the size of a neighborhood used for averaging or the smoothness of a fitted curve, these settings must be chosen carefully.


Compared to a parametric method:
- the main advantage is **flexibility**,without committing to a restrictive functional form, non-parametric methods can capture a wider range of relationships.
- The main disadvantage is also from flexibility, as it generally requires **more training data** to obtain a reliable estimate, especially when there are many predictors.

The distinction is that estimating $f$ is not reduced to estimating a fixed set of coefficients within a predetermined functional form.

## Trade off Between Accuracy and Interpretability


Some of the methods are more restrictive but more understandable, some of the methods are more flexible and less interpretable.

For example, linear regression is a pretty inflexible approach, but it's very easy to understand an estimated linear relationship between the functions.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 5.52.34 PM.jpg|500]]

A more flexible as we can see that can better approximate this data is the `thin plate spline`, but it's harder to interpret why this curves behaves the way it does.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 5.52.57 PM.jpg]]


In general, as the flexibility of a method increases, its interpretability decreases.

![[An Introduction To Statistical Learning.jpg|500]]

[[An Introduction To Statistical Learning.pdf#page=34&rect=115,495,392,662|An Introduction To Statistical Learning, p.34]]

We care about **interpretability** because we may want to understand the relationship a model has learned: which inputs are associated with the outcome, how the predicted outcome changes with those inputs, and how the model arrives at a prediction.

When our goal is **inference**, that understanding is part of the result we want. Interpretability also helps us examine whether the learned relationships are plausible, identify unexpected behavior, and communicate our findings. 

If our main goal is prediction, we may be willing to accept less interpretability in exchange for better predictive performance.

The trade-off arises because **flexibility gives a model more freedom in the relationships it can represent**.

- A restrictive model permits only a limited range of patterns. Those restrictions make the relationship easier to summarize: the model must behave according to a relatively simple structure.

- A flexible model allows the relationship to change more substantially across different inputs. Explaining the model then requires describing more conditions, interactions, and changes in behavior.
